content: >-
  <p>This filing season is different from every one before it — for the first
  time, ITR deadlines are staggered by which form you file, not one common date
  for everyone. That single change alone is behind a fresh wave of missed
  deadlines this year, on top of the mistakes that show up every season
  regardless. Here's the complete list, verified against the actual AY 2026-27
  rules, not last year's assumptions.</p>


  <p>Filing an ITR is technically simple in the sense that the portal walks you through fields one by one — but "the portal accepted it" and "it was filed correctly" are two different things, and the gap between them is exactly where most of these mistakes live. None of what follows requires professional-level tax knowledge to avoid; it requires knowing these specific traps exist before you hit submit.</p>




  <h2 id="deadlines">The deadlines that actually apply to you this year</h2>


  <p>Assuming "July 31 for everyone" is itself the first mistake on this list. For AY 2026-27, the Finance Act 2026 introduced a genuinely new, staggered structure:</p>


  <table>


  <tr><th>Who you are</th><th>Form</th><th>Due date</th></tr>


  <tr><td>Salaried individuals, most people with capital gains</td><td>ITR-1, ITR-2</td><td>31 July 2026</td></tr>


  <tr><td>Business/professional, no audit required</td><td>ITR-3, ITR-4</td><td>31 August 2026 (new — was July 31 in prior years)</td></tr>


  <tr><td>Audit required under Section 44AB</td><td>Any applicable form</td><td>31 October 2026</td></tr>


  <tr><td>International/specified domestic transactions (Form 3CEB)</td><td>Any applicable form</td><td>30 November 2026</td></tr>


  </table>


  <p>Even a one-day delay counts as belated, with real consequences beyond just a late fee — covered in the deadline-specific mistakes below. Belated filing is accepted until 31 December 2026; miss that too, and an updated return (ITR-U) is available up to 48 months from the end of the assessment year, i.e. 31 March 2031 for this AY, though with its own limitations.</p>




  <h2 id="wrong-form">Mistake 1: Filing the wrong ITR form</h2>


  <p>This is still the single most common structural error, and it's not just an inconvenience — a return filed on the wrong form can be treated as defective under Section 139(9), giving you a limited window to fix it before it's treated as if you never filed at all. Common mix-ups: using ITR-1 despite having capital gains above the simplified threshold it allows, using ITR-2 when you actually have business or professional income that requires ITR-3, or filing ITR-4 (presumptive) when your actual turnover or profit margin no longer qualifies for that scheme.</p>


  <p>The fix is simple in principle — match the form to every income source you actually have, not just your primary one — but it's exactly the step people rush past when they're using a slightly outdated understanding of which form "used to" apply to their situation.</p>


  <p>A concrete example: someone who was purely salaried last year and used ITR-1 without issue, but sold some equity or property this year, needs to move to ITR-2 — continuing to file ITR-1 out of habit, because "that's always been my form," is exactly how this mistake happens in practice, not through unfamiliarity with the rules in general.</p>




  <h2 id="reconcile">Mistake 2: Not reconciling with Form 26AS, AIS, and TIS before filing</h2>


  <p>Your Form 16 shows what your employer reported. Form 26AS, AIS (Annual Information Statement), and TIS (Taxpayer Information Summary) show what the <em>department</em> has on record across every source — salary, bank interest, mutual fund transactions, property deals, and more. Filing based on Form 16 alone, without checking these three against it, is how genuine mismatches turn into scrutiny notices months later. See our <a href="form-16-explained.html">Form 16 guide</a> for exactly how these documents relate to each other and what to check specifically.</p>


  <div class="callout"><b>Practical habit:</b> pull all three — 26AS, AIS, TIS — before you open the ITR form itself, not after you've already started filling it in. Reconciling first prevents having to redo sections later.</div>


  <p>A mismatch here doesn't automatically mean something is wrong on your end — sometimes AIS reflects data that's itself incomplete or delayed from a third-party reporting entity. But the responsibility to notice and address the discrepancy before filing sits with you, not the department, and an unexplained mismatch is one of the more common triggers for a follow-up notice after processing.</p>




  <h2 id="missing-income">Mistake 3: Leaving out income you assumed didn't need reporting</h2>


  <p>Several income types get skipped not out of dishonesty but because people genuinely don't realise they're reportable:</p>


  <ul>


  <li><b>Foreign assets and foreign income</b> — reportable under Schedule FA even if no tax is actually due on them, and non-disclosure here falls under the Black Money Act, which carries meaningfully harsher penalties than a standard income tax miss.</li>


  <li><b>Crypto gains</b> — every taxable transaction needs reporting in Schedule VDA, including token-to-token swaps, not just conversions back to rupees.</li>


  <li><b>Exempt income</b> — some exempt income still needs to be disclosed even though no tax applies to it; skipping the disclosure isn't the same as it being genuinely exempt from reporting.</li>


  <li><b>Interest from savings accounts and small fixed deposits</b> — easy to forget when no TDS was deducted on it, but it's still taxable income that needs to be included.</li>


  </ul>

  <h2 id="regime-choice">Mistake 4: Picking a tax regime without actually comparing both</h2>


  <p>The new regime is the default now, which means many people file under it simply by not actively choosing otherwise — not because they actually compared the two and confirmed it's cheaper for them. If you have significant HRA, home loan interest, or 80C/80D deductions, the old regime can still come out ahead by a real margin. This is worth an actual calculation every year, not a one-time decision carried forward on autopilot, since your income, deductions, and the regimes' own rules can all shift year to year.</p>




  <h2 id="form-10iea">Mistake 5: Forgetting Form 10-IEA when opting for the old regime as a business filer</h2>


  <p>If you're a business or professional taxpayer (filing ITR-3 or ITR-4) and want to use the old regime instead of the new default, you must file Form 10-IEA before the due date — this is a separate, mandatory step, not something the ITR form itself handles automatically the way it might for salaried filers. Miss this form, and you can be defaulted into the new regime regardless of which one actually suits you better, with no way to switch back for that year.</p>




  <h2 id="bank-account">Mistake 6: Refund bank account not pre-validated</h2>


  <p>A correctly filed return with a genuine refund due can still sit unpaid indefinitely if the bank account nominated for the refund isn't pre-validated on the income tax portal. This is a five-minute fix done in advance, but it's routinely skipped until someone notices their refund never arrived — worth checking your validated account list before you even start filing, not after submission.</p>




  <h2 id="everify">Mistake 7: Not e-verifying within 30 days</h2>


  <p>Filing your return is only half the process. Without e-verification — via Aadhaar OTP, net banking, a pre-validated bank/demat EVC, or a physically signed ITR-V sent to CPC Bengaluru — within <b>30 days</b> of submission, your return is treated as though it was never filed at all, not just "pending." This 30-day window is the current rule; if you've seen references to 120 days, that's outdated information from several years back that no longer applies.</p>


  <div class="callout good"><b>Simplest fix:</b> e-verify the same day you submit, using Aadhaar OTP if your mobile is linked — it takes under a minute and removes this risk entirely rather than leaving a 30-day window to accidentally miss.</div>




  <h2 id="loss-carry-forward">Mistake 8: Missing the due date and losing loss carry-forward rights</h2>


  <p>This is one of the most expensive mistakes on this list, and one of the least understood. Business losses and most capital losses can only be carried forward to offset future years' income if the return is filed within the original due date under Section 139(1) — filing late, even just a belated return within the extended window, forfeits this right permanently for that year's losses. A ₹3 lakh loss that could have offset future taxable income at your slab rate is a real, calculable cost if the carry-forward right is lost over a technicality of timing.</p>




  <h2 id="audit-threshold">Mistake 9: Not checking whether your books actually need an audit this year</h2>


  <p>Turnover and receipt thresholds for mandatory tax audit under Section 44AB matter here — broadly, above ₹1 crore turnover for business or ₹50 lakh for professional receipts, with a higher ₹10 crore threshold available if at least 95% of your transactions are digital. Assuming last year's audit status still applies without rechecking against this year's actual numbers is a common, avoidable error — and it directly changes which due date applies to you.</p>




  <h2 id="technical">Mistakes that have nothing to do with tax law</h2>


  <p>A genuinely large share of filing problems are purely technical, not substantive:</p>


  <ul>


  <li><b>Session timeouts losing unsaved data</b> — the portal has an idle timeout, and filling a long return in one sitting without saving progress periodically risks losing everything if you step away. Save as draft at each major section.</li>


  <li><b>Browser compatibility issues</b> — the e-filing portal works most reliably on updated versions of standard browsers; older or unusual browser setups occasionally cause forms to render or submit incorrectly without a clear error message.</li>


  <li><b>Filing right at the deadline</b> — portal traffic spikes heavily in the final days before July 31 and August 31, sometimes causing slowdowns or submission failures at exactly the worst moment. Filing even a week early avoids this entirely.</li>


  <li><b>Not downloading the acknowledgment (ITR-V) and confirmation immediately</b> — keep a copy the moment you file, rather than assuming you can always re-download it later without any friction.</li>


  </ul>




  <h2 id="notice-response">If a defective return notice actually arrives</h2>


  <p>A return flagged under Section 139(9) as defective isn't the end of the process — you get a window (typically 15 days, sometimes extended on request) to correct and resubmit it. Ignoring this notice, though, causes the original return to be treated as invalid once that window closes, which can cascade into the same loss-of-carry-forward and belated-filing consequences covered above. Treat a defective return notice as genuinely time-sensitive, not a low-priority formality.</p>




  <h2 id="deduction-claims">Mistake 10: Claiming deductions without matching documentation</h2>


  <p>Claiming 80C, 80D, or HRA deductions based on what you intend to invest or what you estimate you're eligible for, rather than what you can actually document, is a recurring source of trouble at scrutiny time. This is especially common with 80C — people claim the full ₹1.5 lakh limit assuming it'll work out, without actually confirming the investments were made and documented before the financial year closed. A deduction claimed but not substantiated when asked isn't just disallowed; it can also trigger interest and, in some cases, penalty on the resulting shortfall.</p>




  <h2 id="checklist">A pre-submission checklist</h2>


  <ul class="checklist">


  <li>Correct ITR form selected for every income source you actually have</li>


  <li>Form 26AS, AIS, and TIS reconciled against what you're about to file</li>


  <li>Foreign assets, crypto gains, and exempt income all disclosed</li>


  <li>Both tax regimes actually compared with current numbers, not assumed</li>


  <li>Form 10-IEA filed, if applicable to your filer type and regime choice</li>


  <li>Refund bank account pre-validated on the portal</li>


  <li>Correct due date confirmed for your specific form and filer category</li>


  <li>Plan in place to e-verify the same day you submit</li>


  </ul>




  <h2 id="already-mistake">Already filed and found a mistake?</h2>


  <p>A genuine improvement for this assessment year: the deadline to file a revised return under Section 139(5) has been extended to <b>31 March 2027</b> for AY 2026-27, up from the previous 31 December cut-off — giving meaningfully more room to correct an error without penalty, provided the original return was filed on time. If you've missed both the original and belated windows entirely, an updated return (ITR-U) remains available up to 48 months from the end of the assessment year, and — new for this year — losses disclosed in an ITR-U can now be carried forward, which wasn't previously allowed.</p>


  <p>These extended windows are genuinely useful, but they shouldn't change the basic approach: file on time, verify the same day, and treat the pre-submission checklist above as a real step, not a formality to skim past. Every mistake on this list is avoidable with a few extra minutes of checking before you hit submit — the cost of catching them now is minutes; the cost of catching them after a notice arrives is measured in weeks of correspondence, and sometimes real money.</p>
