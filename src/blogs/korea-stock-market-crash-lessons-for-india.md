<p>In June 2026, a 20-something investor in Seoul was sitting on gains that felt like a rocket ship — the kind of returns that make you quit checking your salary and start checking your portfolio ten times a day. By mid-July, that same portfolio was gone. Not down. Gone. Along with somewhere between 32,000 and 46,000 other trading accounts, wiped out in the space of about three weeks. This isn't a hypothetical cautionary tale — it's what actually happened in South Korea this summer, and the mechanics behind it are worth understanding in detail, because the same temptations exist in India, even if the guardrails are different.</p>

<h2 id="the-rally">The rally: how KOSPI doubled in six months</h2>
<p>South Korea's benchmark KOSPI index started 2026 around 4,500 points. By late January it crossed 5,000. By May, 7,000. By June, it briefly touched 9,000 — a roughly 100% gain in six months, one of the sharpest sustained rallies any major global index has seen in years. The engine behind it was unmistakable: AI infrastructure demand, specifically for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) chips, the specialized memory that powers AI accelerators from companies like Nvidia.</p>
<p>South Korea happens to sit at the center of this supply chain. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix together account for roughly 60% of the KOSPI index, and an estimated 35% of global AI hardware profits flow through their HBM dominance. SK Hynix's stock alone gained roughly 300% in the rally; Samsung gained around 170%. The fundamentals weren't fake, either — SK Hynix delivered the single greatest quarterly result in Korean corporate history in Q2 2026: revenue up 257% year-on-year, a 76% operating margin. This was a real, earnings-backed boom, not a meme-stock fantasy — which is exactly what made it so easy for ordinary investors to convince themselves it was different this time.</p>

<h2 id="the-crash">The crash: 25% gone in three weeks</h2>
<p>After hitting its all-time high in late June, KOSPI pulled back roughly 25-27% in just three weeks — officially entering a bear market, and triggering seven separate circuit breakers across the year, an extraordinarily rare frequency for any major market. SK Hynix fell more than 40% from its peak; Samsung shed nearly 30% of its market value. On the single worst day, South Korea's equity market lost approximately $2.18 trillion in value.</p>
<p>The proximate trigger looked almost anticlimactic: SK Hynix reported genuinely spectacular earnings — and the stock fell anyway, nearly 9% on the day results were published. Markets had already priced in perfection; when perfection arrived exactly as expected, there was nothing left to buy on. That single moment of "buy the rumor, sell the news taken to an extreme" was enough to flip sentiment, and once it flipped, leverage did the rest of the damage.</p>

<h2 id="margin-mechanics">What actually happened to the leveraged investors</h2>
<p>Here's the part that turned a normal correction into a genuine crisis for retail investors: leverage. South Korea's margin lending balance hit a record 60 trillion won (roughly $41 billion) in May 2026, and leveraged ETFs — funds designed to deliver 2x or 3x the daily return of an underlying index or stock — were "bought up the moment they listed," according to reporting from the crash. South Korea's capital markets have historically had a notably low barrier to accessing this kind of leverage, with some brokerages packaging high-risk margin trading in language closer to a routine "buy now, pay later" consumer product than a serious financial risk disclosure — a framing that matters, because how a risky product is presented shapes how casually people treat it. On the single worst trading day (13 July), more than 1.2 million leveraged retail accounts triggered margin calls, with approximately 320,000 forcibly liquidated by brokerages. Investors aged 20-30 made up 62% of those affected.</p>
<p>One trader posted publicly about a single-day loss of ₩2.1 billion (about $1.4 million) — a 42% loss rate — after aggressively buying over 200,000 shares of a leveraged product tied to SK Hynix. This wasn't a fringe case; it's illustrative of exactly how the mechanics work at scale.</p>
<p>The demographic pattern is worth sitting with specifically: investors in their 20s and 30s made up nearly two-thirds of those affected — not because younger investors are less capable, but because they're disproportionately the ones with the least capital cushion, the shortest investing track record to draw caution from, and the most exposure to social platforms where leveraged gains get amplified and shared while the eventual losses often don't. A rally that runs for six months straight can make genuinely careful people start to believe caution itself is the risk — that's precisely the psychological setup leverage exploits.</p>

<h2 id="why-leverage-amplifies">Why leverage turns a correction into a wipeout</h2>
<p>This is worth understanding mechanically, not just as a scary headline. A leveraged ETF doesn't just multiply your gains — it multiplies your losses by the same factor, and because these products typically reset their leverage <em>daily</em>, losses compound against you in a way that a simple "2x the index" intuition doesn't capture. A 10% single-day drop in the underlying isn't a 20% loss on a 2x leveraged product held over several days — in a volatile, choppy market, the daily-reset mechanic can produce losses meaningfully worse than a naive doubling, a phenomenon sometimes called "volatility decay."</p>
<p>Margin trading compounds this further. When you buy on margin, you've borrowed money to take a bigger position than your own capital allows. If the position drops, your broker issues a margin call — a demand to deposit more funds immediately to maintain the position. If you can't (or don't, fast enough), the broker force-liquidates your position to recover their loan, regardless of whether that's a good time to sell. This is precisely what turns a market decline into a downward spiral: falling prices trigger margin calls, margin calls force selling, forced selling pushes prices down further, which triggers more margin calls. Korea's 320,000 forced liquidations weren't 320,000 independent bad decisions — they were a mechanical chain reaction.</p>
<p>It's worth being precise about why this specific chain reaction is so much more violent than an ordinary sell-off. In a market with little leverage, falling prices simply mean falling prices — some investors sell because they're worried, others hold, others even buy the dip, and the market finds a new equilibrium through genuinely voluntary decisions. Add heavy leverage, and a chunk of the selling stops being voluntary at all: brokers are contractually required to liquidate regardless of what the investor believes about the stock's future, regardless of whether they'd rather hold on. That forced, mechanical selling adds real downward pressure independent of anyone's actual view on the asset — which is exactly why leveraged crashes tend to fall faster and further than the underlying fundamentals alone would justify, and why they can overshoot on the way down just as sharply as the rally overshot on the way up.</p>

<h2 id="could-it-happen-india">Could this happen in India?</h2>
<p>This is the question that actually matters for you, and the honest answer is: the underlying temptation absolutely exists here too, but India's regulatory structure makes a Korea-scale cascade meaningfully harder to trigger — by design, not by accident.</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Mechanism</th><th>South Korea (as seen in this crash)</th><th>India (SEBI framework, 2026)</th></tr>
<tr><td>Margin trading minimum contribution</td><td>Extremely low barrier; brokerages packaged high-risk margin as routine "buy now, pay later"</td><td>Minimum 25% own contribution required for MTF (Margin Trading Facility) — capping retail leverage at roughly 4x, not the much higher ratios seen in Korea</td></tr>
<tr><td>Which securities can be leveraged</td><td>Broad access, including highly volatile leveraged ETF products</td><td>MTF is restricted to Group 1 securities — SEBI's own high-liquidity, high-stability category. Volatile, speculative names are structurally excluded</td></tr>
<tr><td>Intraday/F&O margin</td><td>Leveraged products bought "the moment they listed," minimal friction</td><td>Peak margin rules require brokers to collect full upfront margin (VaR + Exposure) at all times, checked via random intraday snapshots — effectively capping intraday leverage near 5x, down sharply from pre-2021 practice</td></tr>
<tr><td>Collateral quality</td><td>Not specifically restricted in the same way</td><td>Since April 2026, F&O traders must hold at least 50% of margin collateral in cash or cash-equivalents, not just pledged shares — and RBI now requires 100% collateral for any bank funding to brokers</td></tr>
</table>
<p>None of this makes Indian markets crash-proof — it makes the specific <em>leverage-cascade</em> mechanism that turned Korea's correction into a wipeout structurally harder to replicate at the same scale. SEBI has also been actively tightening further: MTF outstanding positions crossed ₹1.3 trillion by mid-2026, up 50% year-on-year, and the regulator has already proposed raising broker net-worth requirements specifically to build more resilience into the system before a Korea-style event, not after one.</p>
<p>This didn't happen by luck. India's margin framework has been tightened progressively and deliberately since 2020-2021, specifically in response to concerns about exactly this kind of retail leverage cascade — the peak margin rules that cap intraday leverage near 5x were introduced in phases precisely because regulators watched unrestricted leverage create serious retail losses in earlier market cycles and moved to close the gap before it produced a crisis at Korea's scale. The RBI's April 2026 restriction on bank funding for broker proprietary trading is a further, recent example of the same instinct — regulators actively watching for the buildup of systemic leverage risk and tightening ahead of a crisis, not just reacting after one.</p>

<h2 id="fno-warning">The part of this story that does rhyme with India</h2>
<p>Here's where the comparison should make you uncomfortable rather than complacent: India's own F&O (futures and options) retail participation has grown dramatically in recent years, and regulatory tightening has been a direct, repeated response to retail investors losing significant money in leveraged derivatives — this is a well-documented, ongoing pattern in Indian markets, not a hypothetical risk imported from Korea. The specific mechanism differs, but the underlying human behavior — chasing a visible, exciting rally with borrowed money, assuming "this time the trend continues" — is identical. Regulation limits the ceiling on how bad it can get; it doesn't eliminate the temptation, and it can't protect you from a decision to max out whatever leverage is actually available to you.</p>

<h2 id="lessons">What to actually take from this, practically</h2>
<ul>
<li><b>Leverage doesn't just amplify returns — it removes your ability to wait out a downturn.</b> An unleveraged investor who bought SK Hynix at the peak is down on paper, but still owns their shares and can wait for a recovery. A leveraged investor at the same entry point may have been force-liquidated before any recovery had a chance to happen — the loss became permanent by mechanism, not by choice.</li>
<li><b>"The fundamentals are real" doesn't protect you from timing risk.</b> SK Hynix's earnings were genuinely excellent — and the stock still fell nearly 9% on the news, then kept falling. Good fundamentals and good near-term price action are not the same claim.</li>
<li><b>A rally that feels obvious in hindsight rarely feels risky while you're in it.</b> Every account holder who got liquidated in Korea believed the AI trade would keep running — many were right about the long-term story and still lost everything, because leverage doesn't care about being eventually right, only about surviving the interim volatility.</li>
<li><b>Products that reset leverage daily behave differently than simple multiplication suggests.</b> If you don't fully understand how a leveraged or inverse product actually compounds day to day, that's a real, sufficient reason not to hold it, regardless of how compelling the thesis behind the underlying asset is.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="alternative">The unglamorous alternative that actually works</h2>
<p>None of this is an argument against investing in growth themes like AI or semiconductors — it's an argument against doing so with borrowed money and daily-reset leveraged products, chasing a rally that's already made most of its move. A disciplined, unleveraged SIP into a diversified fund, sized against an actual goal and time horizon, doesn't produce headline-grabbing 300% gains — it also doesn't produce a 42% single-day loss on your entire capital. For most people, that trade-off is the entire point of investing sensibly rather than speculating.</p>

<h2 id="circuit-breakers">What circuit breakers actually do (and don't do)</h2>
<p>KOSPI triggered seven circuit breakers across 2026 — automatic trading halts that kick in when an index falls past a specified threshold within a session, designed to give the market a pause to absorb information rather than free-fall on pure panic. It's worth being clear about what this mechanism does and doesn't accomplish: a circuit breaker can prevent a single session from becoming disorderly, but it cannot prevent a leveraged position from being underwater by the time trading resumes, and it cannot stop a margin call that was already triggered before the halt. Indian exchanges (NSE and BSE) have their own circuit breaker framework, broadly similar in purpose — useful context if you ever see one triggered and wonder what it actually changes for your own leveraged position: less than you might hope.</p>

<h2 id="before-leverage">Questions worth asking before you ever use margin or a leveraged product</h2>
<ul class="checklist">
<li>Do I understand exactly how much of my own capital I'm contributing versus how much is borrowed?</li>
<li>Have I checked what specific price movement would trigger a margin call on this exact position?</li>
<li>If leveraged ETF: do I understand that daily-reset compounding means holding period matters, not just direction?</li>
<li>Am I using leverage because the underlying thesis is genuinely strong, or because the recent rally made unleveraged gains feel too slow?</li>
<li>Could I survive this position being force-liquidated at the worst possible moment — financially and psychologically?</li>
<li>Am I checking my broker's specific margin call and liquidation policy, not just assuming it works like a stock I've held before?</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>Korea's crash isn't a story about a bad market or a broken thesis — the AI memory chip boom behind the rally was, and largely still is, a genuine, earnings-backed trend. It's a story about what leverage does to good decisions made at the wrong moment. The investors who lost everything weren't necessarily wrong about where the market was headed long-term; they were exposed to a mechanism that doesn't allow you to be wrong about timing even slightly. That's the actual lesson worth carrying into how you invest in India, regardless of which market or theme eventually captures the same kind of excitement here next.</p>

<div class="callout"><b>Worth remembering:</b> nobody posts about their disciplined, boring SIP on a public forum the way Korea's leveraged traders posted their gains during the rally. Survivorship and visibility bias mean the loud, exciting stories you see are disproportionately the ones that eventually end badly — the quiet, steady ones simply don't make for dramatic posts.</div>
