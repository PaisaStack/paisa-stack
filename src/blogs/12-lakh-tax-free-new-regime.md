<div class="callout good" id="short-answer"><b>Short answer:</b> In Tax Year 2026-27, if your <b>taxable income is ₹12 lakh or less</b>, your income tax is <b>zero</b> in the new regime. A rebate of up to <b>₹60,000</b> wipes out the slab tax. Salaried people also get a ₹75,000 standard deduction, so a <b>salary of up to ₹12.75 lakh is tax-free</b>. Just above the line, marginal relief means you never pay more tax than the income you earned above ₹12 lakh. The rebate does <b>not</b> cover capital gains taxed at special rates, and NRIs don't get it.</div>

<p>"₹12 lakh tak tax free" is the most quoted line from Budget 2025, and it still holds for Tax Year 2026-27. But many people misread it. Some think ₹12 lakh is a new exemption limit (it isn't). Others think their stock market gains are covered too (they aren't). And many panic at ₹12.1 lakh, fearing a ₹60,000 bill.</p>
<p>This guide explains exactly how the zero-tax band works, with six examples in rupees, so you know where you stand before filing.</p>

<h2 id="how-it-works">How ₹12 lakh becomes tax-free</h2>
<p>The ₹12 lakh figure isn't a slab. The new regime slabs still start taxing income above ₹4 lakh:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Taxable income</th><th>Rate</th><th>Tax in this band</th></tr>
<tr><td>Up to ₹4,00,000</td><td>Nil</td><td>₹0</td></tr>
<tr><td>₹4,00,001 to ₹8,00,000</td><td>5%</td><td>₹20,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>₹8,00,001 to ₹12,00,000</td><td>10%</td><td>₹40,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>₹12,00,001 to ₹16,00,000</td><td>15%</td><td></td></tr>
<tr><td>₹16,00,001 to ₹20,00,000</td><td>20%</td><td></td></tr>
<tr><td>₹20,00,001 to ₹24,00,000</td><td>25%</td><td></td></tr>
<tr><td>Above ₹24,00,000</td><td>30%</td><td></td></tr>
</table>
<p>So the slab tax on ₹12 lakh is ₹20,000 + ₹40,000 = <b>₹60,000</b>. Then the <b>rebate</b> kicks in. It's Section 87A under the old 1961 Act and <b>Section 156</b> under the Income-tax Act 2025, which applies from 1 April 2026. It gives a rebate of up to ₹60,000 to resident individuals whose taxable income is ₹12 lakh or less. Tax minus rebate is zero, and cess on zero is zero.</p>
<p>That's why it matters whether you're at ₹11.9 lakh or ₹12.1 lakh. The rebate is all or nothing at ₹12 lakh, softened only by marginal relief, which we cover below.</p>

<h2 id="salary-1275">Why salaried people get ₹12.75 lakh</h2>
<p>Salaried employees and pensioners get a <b>₹75,000 standard deduction</b> in the new regime before tax is calculated. So:</p>
<p><b>₹12,75,000 salary − ₹75,000 standard deduction = ₹12,00,000 taxable income = zero tax.</b></p>
<p>Freelancers, consultants and business owners don't get the standard deduction. For them the line is ₹12 lakh of income. Under 44ADA presumptive taxation, only 50% of receipts counts as income, so a freelancer can bill up to ₹24 lakh and stay in the zero-tax band. Our <a href="advance-tax-for-freelancers.html">advance tax guide for freelancers</a> has examples.</p>
<p>Remember that "salary" here means taxable salary, not CTC. Employer PF and gratuity are removed first, so a CTC of around ₹13.9 lakh can still mean zero tax. Our <a href="10-lpa-in-hand-salary.html">10 LPA in-hand salary guide</a> shows the full breakup.</p>

<h2 id="examples">6 worked examples (Tax Year 2026-27, new regime)</h2>

<h3>1. Priya: salary ₹12,00,000</h3>
<p>Taxable income: ₹12,00,000 − ₹75,000 = ₹11,25,000. Slab tax: ₹20,000 + ₹32,500 = ₹52,500. Rebate: ₹52,500. <b>Tax: ₹0.</b></p>

<h3>2. Rahul: salary ₹12,75,000</h3>
<p>Taxable income: exactly ₹12,00,000. Slab tax: ₹60,000. Rebate: ₹60,000. <b>Tax: ₹0.</b> This is the highest salary with zero tax.</p>

<h3>3. Anita: salary ₹13,00,000</h3>
<p>Taxable income: ₹12,25,000. Slab tax: ₹60,000 + 15% of ₹25,000 = ₹63,750. No rebate, because she's above ₹12 lakh. But marginal relief caps her tax at the excess over ₹12 lakh, which is ₹25,000. Add 4% cess: <b>tax ₹26,000</b>, not ₹66,300.</p>

<h3>4. Vikram: freelancer with ₹12,50,000 income</h3>
<p>No standard deduction, so taxable income is ₹12,50,000. Slab tax: ₹67,500. Marginal relief caps it at ₹50,000. With cess, <b>tax is ₹52,000</b>. Had his income been ₹12,00,000, his tax would be zero.</p>

<h3>5. Sneha: salary ₹10,75,000 + ₹1,00,000 short-term equity gains</h3>
<p>After the standard deduction, her taxable salary is ₹10,00,000. The slab tax on it is ₹40,000, which the rebate fully cancels. But short-term gains on listed shares and equity funds are taxed at a special <b>20%</b> rate, and the rebate can't be used against that. Tax on gains: ₹20,000 + 4% cess = <b>₹20,800</b>. Her total income is only ₹11 lakh, yet she still pays tax.</p>

<h3>6. Arvind: salary ₹15,00,000</h3>
<p>Taxable income: ₹14,25,000. Slab tax: ₹60,000 + 15% of ₹2,25,000 = ₹93,750. That's well below the ₹2,25,000 excess, so marginal relief doesn't help. With cess, <b>tax is ₹97,500</b>.</p>

<h2 id="marginal-relief">Earning just above ₹12 lakh: marginal relief</h2>
<p>Without relief, earning ₹12,10,000 instead of ₹12,00,000 would push your tax from ₹0 to over ₹61,500. That would mean ₹10,000 more income costing you ₹61,500 more tax. Marginal relief prevents this. In the new regime, <b>your tax before cess can't be more than the amount by which your taxable income exceeds ₹12 lakh</b>.</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Salary</th><th>Taxable income</th><th>Slab tax</th><th>Tax after marginal relief + cess</th></tr>
<tr><td>₹12,75,000</td><td>₹12,00,000</td><td>₹60,000</td><td>₹0</td></tr>
<tr><td>₹12,85,000</td><td>₹12,10,000</td><td>₹61,500</td><td>₹10,400</td></tr>
<tr><td>₹13,00,000</td><td>₹12,25,000</td><td>₹63,750</td><td>₹26,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>₹13,25,000</td><td>₹12,50,000</td><td>₹67,500</td><td>₹52,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>₹13,45,588</td><td>₹12,70,588</td><td>₹70,588</td><td>₹73,412 (relief ends here)</td></tr>
<tr><td>₹14,00,000</td><td>₹13,25,000</td><td>₹78,750</td><td>₹81,900</td></tr>
</table>
<p>Marginal relief runs out at a taxable income of about <b>₹12.7 lakh</b> (a salary of about ₹13.46 lakh). Above that, normal slab tax is lower than the excess, so you just pay slab tax.</p>
<p>One small quirk: the cap applies before cess. So in the relief zone, your tax plus 4% cess is slightly more than the extra income. Earning ₹25,000 above the line costs ₹26,000 in tax. It's a reason to plan, not to refuse a raise.</p>

<h2 id="catch">The catch: capital gains and special-rate income</h2>
<p>The rebate only reduces tax on income taxed at <b>normal slab rates</b>: salary, business, freelance, rent, interest and similar. It does <b>not</b> reduce tax on income taxed at special rates, including:</p>
<ul>
<li><b>Short-term gains on listed shares and equity mutual funds</b>, taxed at 20%.</li>
<li><b>Long-term gains on listed shares and equity mutual funds</b>, taxed at 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year.</li>
<li><b>Crypto and other virtual digital assets</b>, taxed at a flat 30%.</li>
<li><b>Lottery, game show and online gaming winnings</b>, taxed at 30%.</li>
</ul>
<p>So an investor with ₹8 lakh of salary income and ₹3 lakh of long-term equity gains pays zero on the salary, but 12.5% on ₹1.75 lakh of gains (above the ₹1.25 lakh exemption). That's ₹21,875 plus cess, or ₹22,750. Our guide to <a href="sip-taxation-explained.html">how SIPs are taxed</a> explains the ₹1.25 lakh exemption in detail.</p>
<div class="callout"><b>If gains push you above ₹12 lakh:</b> whether you still get the rebate on your slab income, when capital gains take your total income above ₹12 lakh, has been interpreted differently. If you're in that position, check the result in the ITR utility before filing, or speak to a CA.</div>

<h2 id="who-cant">Who doesn't get the ₹12 lakh rebate</h2>
<ul>
<li><b>NRIs:</b> the rebate is only for resident individuals. Resident but not ordinarily resident (RNOR) individuals do qualify.</li>
<li><b>HUFs, firms and companies:</b> only individuals get it.</li>
<li><b>People who opt for the old regime:</b> the old regime has its own, much smaller rebate.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="old-regime">Old regime vs new regime at ₹12 lakh</h2>
<p>In the old regime, the rebate is only up to <b>₹12,500</b>, and only if taxable income is ₹5 lakh or less. At ₹12 lakh of taxable income, old regime tax is ₹12,500 (5% band) + ₹1,00,000 (20% band) + ₹60,000 (30% on ₹2 lakh) = ₹1,72,500. That's ₹1,79,400 with cess.</p>
<p>To bring old regime tax to zero at a ₹12.75 lakh salary, you'd need deductions of ₹7.25 lakh (₹12.75 lakh − ₹50,000 standard deduction − ₹5 lakh). Very few people have that much in 80C, 80D, HRA and home loan interest combined. That's why almost everyone earning up to about ₹12.75 lakh should stay in the new regime. The <a href="../calculators/income-tax-calculator.html">Income Tax calculator</a> will show the exact difference for your numbers.</p>

<h2 id="tips">How to stay in the zero-tax band (legally)</h2>
<ol>
<li><b>Ask for employer NPS.</b> Your employer's NPS contribution of up to 14% of basic is deductible in the new regime under Section 80CCD(2). If you're just above the line, restructuring part of your CTC into employer NPS can bring taxable income back under ₹12 lakh.</li>
<li><b>Time your capital gains.</b> Use the ₹1.25 lakh yearly LTCG exemption on equity, and avoid short-term selling where you can.</li>
<li><b>Freelancers: check 44ADA.</b> Declaring 50% of receipts as income often keeps you under ₹12 lakh.</li>
<li><b>Check your TDS early.</b> If your salary is ₹12.75 lakh or less and your employer is deducting TDS, ask HR whether they've put you in the old regime.</li>
<li><b>Use the <a href="../calculators/salary-in-hand-calculator.html">salary in-hand calculator</a></b> before you negotiate an offer near the line.</li>
</ol>
